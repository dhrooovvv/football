More than a Hack 2027 — Phase 1: Ball
Possession
Problem brief: what is required, how it is scored, what exists already
Prepared 3 Nov 2026 from the organisers' repository (all seven .md files, the scorer, the
baseline, the public ground-truth file) plus outside research. Nothing here uses the gated
match data, which we do not have yet.
1. The problem in one paragraph
One full LaLiga match (Barcelona vs Athletic Club, 2024-25, matchday 2), 199,319 video
frames at 25 fps. For every frame, output one label: H (home, Barcelona, in possession,
ball alive), A (away in possession, ball alive), D (dead ball), or X (abstain). The input is
automatic, uncorrected tracking of 22 players and the ball, automatic tactical tagging,
per-frame camera homography and two videos. The score is computed against a private,
hidden frame-by-frame ground truth. The ranking metric is spellF1 , which asks "how
many of the match's real possessions did you recover as whole possessions?", not "how
many frames did you get right?". There is no jury and no presentation. The score decides
the ranking.

Tracking (ATD). Metrica's computer-vision output in FIFA/EPTS format: x, y and speed for
each track slot, plus the ball, per frame. Coordinates are normalised 0–1 (pitch about 105.3
× 68 m). No player identities: the 46 slots are tracklets that appear, vanish and swap
identity. You get positions and team membership only, so any model has to be collective
rather than per-player. About 20.5 tracked objects per graded frame on average,
occasionally more than 22 because of duplicates. Missing values are NaN. Judging from
the baseline and loader code, the ATD carries no ball-status or ball-owner field, only
position and speed. Confirm this when the data is in hand.
Smart Tagging. Metrica's automatic phase and set-piece annotation: 638 intervals with
codes (Build Up, Progression, Set Pieces, Attacking/Defensive Transition, Long Ball, Goals,
and so on) and tags (Team, Half, Type such as Throw-in/Corner/Goal Kick/Free Kick/Kick-
off, Side, and others). Inferred, not verified: it contains false positives and negatives. 36
instances have Team = N/A , including every throw-in. The intervals are phases, not
possessions. Time base: seconds from the first video frame, frame = round(seconds ×
25) .
Homography. One 3×3 matrix per frame mapping normalised pitch coordinates to pixels,
with valid and reprojection_error columns. This is what ties the tracking to the video
if you want to look at pixels.

5. What the labels mean
H / A : that team is in possession and the ball is alive. D : the ball is not in play. X : you
decline to answer; on frames with no ground truth it is never scored.
Three states only in the truth. No "contested", "transition" or "unknown". While play is
live, one team always holds possession; in a loose or contested phase, possession stays
with the last team that had it.
A possession is a maximal run of one team with the ball alive. H H H A A is two
possessions. H H D D H H is two possessions with a stoppage between them, not
one. The ranking metric is built on this definition.
Dead ball is a stoppage, never a possession, and dead-ball ownership is deliberately
not asked. The tracking names a team even when the ball is dead (restarter or last
toucher); the truth discards that.
How the truth was produced: mechanically from corrected tracking, not from a human
watching video and not from event data. A frame is D where the corrected tracking
marks the ball dead, otherwise it takes the team the corrected tracking names as

"Controlled" possession is the ambiguous part. Not a change of possession: a simple
touch without control; a deflection that does not intercept a pass; an aerial duel won
without subsequent control. The organisers suggest proxies such as a player keeping
the ball for a few seconds, or more than two consecutive passes by a team. If your
reading disagrees with the ground truth, the ground truth is right.
What the public 5 minutes show structurally (inspected only to understand the label
semantics, not to tune): 17 possessions plus 4 dead-ball stretches in 7,500 frames.
Possessions range from under a second (a 19-frame H between two A runs, and a 33-
frame one) to over 40 seconds. Dead stretches run roughly 5 to 20 seconds. So the truth
contains genuine very short possessions as well as long ones. That is exactly why a single
fixed smoothing window is crude, and why hunting for the "right" window on this sample
is the trap the organisers warn about.
6. How it is scored (exactly)
The scorer in evaluation/run_evaluation.py is the official one. The definitions live in
evaluation/possession_metrics.py (about 720 lines of numpy), and if the prose and
the code disagree, the code wins.
spellF1 (ranking metric).
Predicted possessions (maximal runs of H or A ) are matched one-to-one to true
possessions by temporal IoU.
F1 of that matching is averaged over IoU thresholds 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7.
Range is a true [0, 1]; any constant guess scores exactly 0.
Sensitivity to timing: perfect but 1 frame late = 1.0000; 3 frames late = 0.9818; 8 frames
= 0.8933; a full second late = 0.6539.
Noise band: differences under about 0.04 are noise on the full match.
Consequence: every true possession counts once, regardless of length. Missing a 1-
second possession costs as much as missing a 40-second one. Flickering (many tiny
predicted possessions) wrecks precision; over-smoothing (merging real possessions)
wrecks recall.

macroF1 (first tie-break). Unweighted mean of per-class F1 for H , A , D over every
graded frame. Floor is about 0.16, not 0. Differences under about 0.01 are noise. It is nearly
blind to timing offsets and to the possession count.
How X is scored.
macroF1 counts an X on a graded frame as a miss.
spellF1 never sees X : before matching, the scorer forward-fills each X with your
last non- X label in the same half (back-fills at the head of a half).
So on the ranking metric X means "repeat my previous answer". Going quiet inside a
possession is free (and safer than a guess that might split it). Going quiet across a
hand-over is expensive, because your old label bleeds into the new state. At a
transition you should guess.
The published baselines (nearest tracked player to the ball, then a majority vote over a
window; the same algorithm four times):
baseline_smooth50 at spellF1 0.2433 is the published floor. The public-window column
shows the trap: ±125 looks best on the 5 minutes and is worse than ±50 on the full match.
One missed possession in 17 moves spellF1 by about 6 points there. The organisers
deliberately do not publish the full match's possession count or mean duration, so those
cannot be tuned against.
7. Why this is hard
1. The ball is missing on 41% of graded frames, disproportionately in crowded boxes,
aerial duels and tackles, which are the moments that matter. The baseline labels these
frames D , which is a large part of why its dead-ball class is weak.
2. Tracking is noisy: gaps, fragmented tracks, identity switches, no player IDs, occasional
duplicate objects.

3. "Controlled possession" is a judgement, and it is implemented by the provider's
corrected-tracking pipeline whose logic we cannot see.
4. Possessions are intervals. The metric rewards getting boundaries right at roughly IoU
≥ 0.3–0.7. Per-frame accuracy hides the failure mode completely (the organisers say a
system can score near 0.97 per frame while reporting far more possessions than
occurred).
5. Dead-ball detection decides spell structure. H H D D H H is two possessions;
mislabelling a stoppage as live play merges them, and the reverse splits them.
6. No usable validation signal. 17 possessions is the only ground truth, and we are
forbidden from tuning on it. Any model selection has to come from other games or
from unlabelled consistency checks.
8. Open source and prior work
What I found, grouped by how useful it is for this problem. I only opened the repository
itself; everything below is from search results and should be verified before we depend
on it.
Directly the same task
Borghesi, Costa, Morra, Lamberti (2023), "Using Temporal Convolutional Networks
to estimate ball possession in soccer games", Expert Systems with Applications
223. Classifies each moment as dead, home-owned or away-owned from tracking data,
as a ternary prediction or as dead/alive then team. Reported 86.2% possession
accuracy (+7.3% over prior rule-based methods) and 89.2% dead/alive accuracy
(+33.2%), on a different dataset. Those are frame accuracies, not spellF1 , so they are
not comparable to our number, but the problem formulation is the closest match I
found. PDF: iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2976470.
Vidal-Codina, Evans, El Fakir, Billingham (2022), "Automatic event detection in
football using tracking data", Sports Engineering 25:18 (arXiv 2202.00804, CC-BY). A
possession model (which player has the ball each frame, plus player configurations
while the ball is dead) followed by an event model (passes, shots, receptions,
interceptions, set pieces). Reports 90%+ detection rate across tournaments and
providers. Good for the rule-based side: how to use the laws of football to define
restarts and ball-in-play.

9. Design axes (not yet decisions)
The organisers say there is no intended solution. The choices I see:
1. Per-frame signal: nearest-player-to-ball is the baseline. Better: distance and velocity
features for the ball and surrounding players, ball speed changes, possession
"influence" fields, ball height proxies where available.
2. Handling the 41% missing ball: keep going from the last known state, interpolate, or
infer ball location from player configuration (Kim et al.). Remember X forward-fills in
spellF1 .
3. Sequence structure: a state model over {H, A, D} with duration priors (HMM / semi-
Markov / CRF / Viterbi with minimum-duration constraints), or a learned sequence
model (TCN, as in Borghesi et al.). This addresses the single-window flicker/merge
tradeoff directly.
4. Dead-ball detection as its own sub-problem, using ball-speed signatures, ball out of
pitch bounds, all-players-stationary patterns, and Smart Tagging set-piece intervals as
restart evidence.
5. Smart Tagging as priors: every phase has a team, every set piece is a restart. Free, but
inferred and with N/A teams.
6. Video / homography: possible but expensive, and unclear it pays back.
7. Training data: pretrain on other games (IDSSE first), run no hand-labelling on this game.

12. Can we use any model? (LLM APIs, fine-tuning, Jev)
Short answer: any method and any model is allowed, commercial or open, with no
requirement to fine-tune anything. Nothing is scored except the CSV, and using LLMs is
explicitly not an evaluation criterion this year. The winner is whoever has the best
spellF1 on the hidden full match.
The catch: the competition data (tracking, Smart Tagging, homography, video, or any
extract of them) must not be sent to any third-party hosted or cloud LLM. So Claude,
ChatGPT or any other hosted API cannot receive match data. Fine: an assistant as a
coding helper with no data pasted, and open-source models run locally on our own
machine.

Why an LLM is a poor fit anyway: the input is about 199,000 frames of numbers and the
output is a stream of H/A/D labels. That is a time-series problem. Numeric sequence
models and rules handle it better, and per-frame API calls would be slow, costly and non-
reproducible, while the repository must regenerate the identical file.
Jev (TypeSafe "System One" model)
Jev, announced in September 2026, returns typed probabilistic decisions instead of text:
you send a block of state and a question with fixed answers (for example H/A/D) and get
calibrated probabilities back in one parallel pass, with reported latency of 70–500 ms. All
published numbers are vendor-reported with no large independent reproduction yet, and
it is in early access.
Verdict: not as the core of the solution.
1. Data rule: Jev is a hosted API, so every call would ship match data to TypeSafe. It is not
a chat model, but the clause targets exactly that data flow. If we still want it, ask the
organisers via an issue first (RULES.md says to ask before building near a line).
2. Reproducibility: a vendor API in early access can change, rate-limit or be unavailable
when the organisers re-run our repository. A local model gives the identical file every
time.
3. Fit: possessions are intervals, and the metric rewards clean boundaries over 199,319
frames. A per-frame classifier, however fast, still needs a sequence layer on top.
4. Unproven accuracy here: its benchmark is about 68% agreement across four business
workflows (incident response, invoices and similar), nothing like football geometry.
5. No advantage over local models: a gradient-boosted model or small TCN trained on
other games also outputs probabilities, can be calibrated, runs locally and costs
nothing.
Optional experiment only, and only on non-competition data.
13. Recommended approach (untested; best reasoning so far)
Core idea: spellF1 rewards clean possession intervals, so the pipeline needs both a
good per-frame decision and a decoder that preserves possessions.
1. Per-frame model, local, outputs probabilities for H / A / D. A gradient-boosted model
or a small TCN. Inputs: ball position and speed, distance to the nearest players of each
team, number of players near the ball, ball-visible flag, time since the ball was last seen,

ball position relative to the pitch edges. Normalise attacking direction per half so the
model sees one consistent geometry.
2. Sequence decoder on top. An HMM or semi-Markov model with Viterbi decoding,
using the per-frame probabilities as evidence, with duration priors and switching
penalties. One noisy frame then cannot split a possession, and a real hand-over still
gets through. This is what the baseline's fixed smoothing window crudely
approximates, and I expect it to be the largest spellF1 gain.
Training and validation without breaking the rules:
Use the IDSSE open data (7 Bundesliga matches with possession and ball-status
codes, readable with Floodlight or kloppy).
Degrade it to resemble our input: drop the ball on about 41% of frames in realistic
patterns, and add gaps and identity switches.
Leave-one-match-out: train on six matches, test on the seventh, rotate through all
seven.
Score each held-out match with the organisers' own possession_metrics.py .
This gives a real spellF1 signal for choosing decoder settings (minimum possession
length, switch penalty) without touching the public 5 minutes or hand-labelling the
target game.
Caveat: IDSSE's possession conventions may differ from Metrica's, so treat the score
as a relative guide, not a prediction of the final number.
Add-ons, in order of expected value:
1. Dead-ball handling: a separate dead/alive signal (ball out of bounds, ball slowing to a
stop, players stationary) plus Smart Tagging set-piece intervals as restart evidence.
Dead stretches define where possessions end and start.
2. Missing-ball handling: hold the last state through short gaps. Because X forward-fills
under spellF1 , abstain inside a possession but guess at likely hand-overs. A learned
ball-position estimate from player positions (Kim et al.) is a later upgrade.
3. Free wins: label the 2,934 pre-kickoff second-half frames as D , and write X on
ungraded segments.
Skip for now: video and homography (large effort, unclear payoff), the broadcast feed (no
frame mapping provided), and any hosted API.
